Vehicular Ad-hoc Network (VANET)s support modern intelligent transportation applications such as real-time object detection and collaborative traffic prediction, which demand computational resources beyond onboard vehicle capabilities. Fog computing alleviates this by offloading tasks to Roadside Units (RSUs); however, frequent submissions with repetitive patterns increase transceiving overhead and strain network resources, especially due to redundant tasks among neighboring vehicles. \section{Introduction}

\IEEEPARstart{T}{he} \textcolor{blue}{rapid advancement of connected vehicle technologies has transformed modern transportation infrastructure, with \gls{VANET} serving as the backbone for next-generation intelligent transportation systems. By facilitating seamless communication between vehicles and the roadside infrastructure, VANETs enable critical applications ranging from autonomous driving coordination to real-time hazard detection and cooperative traffic optimization \cite{engoulou2014vanet}. The computational demands of these applications continue to escalate, requiring sophisticated processing capabilities that exceed the limited onboard resources available in individual vehicles.}

\textcolor{blue}{To address this computational resource limitation, fog computing has emerged as a promising paradigm that deploys processing capabilities at Roadside Units (RSU) and fog servers positioned throughout the network \cite{mouradian2017comprehensive}. By bringing computational resources closer to vehicles, fog computing reduces communication latency compared to traditional cloud-based solutions while providing sufficient processing power for complex vehicular applications. However, a critical observation reveals that many computational tasks in VANETs exhibit significant redundancy. Multiple vehicles frequently request identical computations—such as optimal route calculations for common destinations, traffic density estimations for shared road segments, or environmental data processing from overlapping sensor coverage areas. Repeatedly executing these duplicate tasks consumes substantial computational resources and energy at fog nodes \cite{wu2021design}. Furthermore, many vehicular tasks involve processing large data volumes such as high-resolution sensor data, video streams from onboard cameras, or LiDAR point clouds. Repetitively offloading these data-intensive tasks to fog nodes not only consumes substantial wireless bandwidth but also incurs significant transmission energy overhead at both vehicles and RSUs, exacerbating the energy waste problem in resource-constrained vehicular networks \cite{safaei2022elite}.}

\textcolor{blue}{While traditional content caching techniques help reduce latency and improve resource efficiency, they do not eliminate redundant computations, as each request still requires full task execution. Likewise, service caching enables execution at edge nodes, but every incoming request must still be processed from scratch. On the other hand, task result caching offers an elegant solution by storing outputs of previously executed tasks and reusing them for subsequent identical requests. By preventing repeated task execution, caching conserves both energy and computational resources at fog nodes and vehicles, freeing these resources to execute other critical tasks and improving overall system throughput. When a vehicle submits a task request, the system first queries the cache; upon a cache hit, the stored result is returned immediately without re-execution, dramatically reducing response time, energy consumption, and computational overhead. However, implementing effective caching in VANETs introduces unique challenges. The continuous movement of vehicles creates highly dynamic access patterns, where task popularity fluctuates based on geographic location, time of day, and traffic conditions \cite{domingos2016data}. In addition, the limited storage capacity of fog nodes demands intelligent cache management strategies, while the heterogeneous nature of vehicular tasks—characterized by diverse computational requirements, varying urgency levels, and different data volumes—necessitates sophisticated decision-making beyond simple frequency-based heuristics.}

\textcolor{blue}{Traditional caching policies such as \gls{LRU}, Least Frequently Used, and First-In-First-Out (FIFO) rely on static rules that cannot adapt to the unique dynamics of vehicular networks. In contrast, machine learning techniques present a promising alternative by enabling data-driven, adaptive caching strategies. Specifically, \gls{FL} enables distributed model training across multiple fog nodes while preserving data privacy, analyzing task characteristics to predict future popularity, and proactively populating caches \cite{yu2015cooperative}. Complementing this, \gls{RL} offers a framework for optimizing cache eviction decisions through continuous interaction with the system environment, dynamically adjusting to real-time conditions and balancing multiple competing goals such as maximizing cache hit rates and minimizing task completion times. However, existing research predominantly explores FL and RL independently for VANET caching, overlooking the potential synergies from combining these complementary approaches. Furthermore, most of the existing studies concentrate on content caching for data dissemination rather than task result caching for computational offloading, which possesses unique attributes including execution deadlines, resource consumption profiles, and priority classifications. Furthermore, while the integration of \gls{SDN} with VANETs offers centralized visibility and programmable control, creating opportunities for coordinated caching decisions across distributed fog infrastructure \cite{misra2019soft}, yet leveraging SDN for intelligent, learning-based cache management in vehicular fog computing remains largely unexplored.}

\textcolor{blue}{Motivated by the above, this paper introduces \textbf{CoCa (Collaborative Cache Management)}, a novel hybrid intelligent caching framework for task result management in Software-Defined VANETs that synergistically combines \gls{FL} for collaborative task pattern prediction with \gls{RL} for adaptive cache replacement control. CoCa employs an FL-based prediction module that analyzes task characteristics across distributed RSUs to forecast task similarity patterns and result reusability, enabling proactive cache management without compromising data privacy. Complementing this, a DQN-based RL agent continuously monitors system conditions and dynamically adjusts cache replacement decisions to optimize multiple objectives, including cache hit rate, task success rate, completion time, and energy efficiency. This dual-learning design leverages FL's strength in collaborative pattern recognition with RL's capability for dynamic optimization under uncertainty.}

The key contributions of CoCa are as follows:}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textcolor{blue}{\textbf{Novel Hybrid FL+DRL Caching Framework}: A two-phase caching framework, CoCa, is proposed that synergistically combines FL for proactive cache pre-placement with Deep Reinforcement Learning for adaptive cache eviction in Software-Defined VANETs. The FL phase employs a Multi-Layer Perceptron trained via Federated Averaging (FedAvg) across distributed RSUs, predicting task popularity through a 12-dimensional feature vector encompassing request frequency, temporal popularity decay, task priority, data size, vehicle speed, RSU load, and one-hot task type encoding—enabling privacy-preserving collaborative pattern recognition without centralizing raw data. The DRL phase deploys a Double Deep Q-Network (Double DQN) with experience replay and target network stabilization, which is particularly suited to the cache eviction problem as it handles discrete action selection among eviction strategies (LRU, LFU, no-operation) under a continuous, high-dimensional state space without requiring an explicit environment model. This model-free property is critical in VANETs where transition dynamics are non-stationary due to vehicle mobility. The two phases complement each other: FL captures long-term spatiotemporal popularity patterns for anticipatory caching, while DRL reacts to short-term state fluctuations for real-time eviction optimization.}

    \item \textcolor{blue}{\textbf{Vehicle-Identity-Agnostic Task Result Caching}: A hash-based cache key generation mechanism is introduced that derives keys solely from task intrinsic parameters—including task type, priority, and quantized data size—independent of vehicle identity. This design enables both intra-vehicle reuse (same vehicle, repeated requests) and cross-vehicle reuse (different vehicles, identical computations), maximizing cache utility with $O(1)$ lookup complexity. Unlike content caching approaches that associate cached items with specific requesters, this vehicle-agnostic design exploits the inherent task redundancy among neighboring vehicles in dense urban VANET environments, significantly amplifying the effective cache capacity without additional storage overhead.}
    
    \item \textcolor{blue}{\textbf{Distributed Cache Management with SDN Coordination}: A multi-level distributed cache management architecture is designed where the SDN controller hosts both the FL advisor and DRL agent, maintaining global visibility of cache states across all RSUs. The architecture implements three coordination mechanisms: (i) admission control that filters low-frequency tasks to prevent cache pollution, (ii) hot replication that automatically propagates high-demand results to neighboring RSUs for mobility-resilient access, and (iii) cross-RSU forwarding that queries remote caches on local misses with automatic local replication upon hit. This layered design maximizes cache utilization under limited RSU storage while adapting to vehicle mobility patterns through SDN-coordinated decisions.}

    \item \textcolor{blue}{\textbf{Energy-Aware Caching Optimization}: A formal energy consumption model is developed that captures three components—vehicle-side transmission energy ($E_{\text{comm}}^{\text{veh}}$), fog-side communication energy ($E_{\text{comm}}^{\text{fog}}$), and fog-side computational energy ($E_{\text{comp}}^{\text{fog}}$)—enabling CoCa to quantify and minimize the energy impact of caching decisions. Upon cache hits, computational energy is entirely eliminated ($E_{\text{comp}}^{\text{fog}} = 0$), leaving only communication overhead. By integrating this energy model into the RL reward structure, CoCa jointly optimizes cache performance and energy consumption, achieving 57.7\% lower energy usage compared to the best-performing baseline—a critical advantage for battery-powered vehicles and resource-constrained RSU deployments.}

    \item \textcolor{blue}{\textbf{Comprehensive Performance Validation}: An extensive set of experimental validation, including systematic parameter optimization, ablation studies, and comparative analysis against state-of-the-art baselines, is conducted. Accordingly, CoCa has achieved 11.0 percentage points higher cache hit rate, 16.4\% lower completion time, and 57.7\% lower energy consumption compared to the best-performing baseline. Ablation studies confirm the complementary contributions of both FL and DRL components, while systematic optimization of the popularity decay parameter ($\lambda = 0.2$, corresponding to approximately 5-second relevance half-life) reveals the rapid temporal dynamics of vehicular task patterns and provides design insights for future VANET caching systems.}
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Studies}

\noindent Task offloading and result caching in vehicular fog computing have evolved significantly to address computational resource constraints, dynamic mobility, and varying task patterns. Traditional reactive policies process tasks independently without reusing results, leading to redundant computation and increased latency in highly dynamic vehicular environments \cite{zhang2018caching}.

Early machine learning approaches employed centralized deep learning for task pattern prediction \cite{chen2019deep}, where centralized servers train neural networks using historical task execution data. While demonstrating improved prediction accuracy, these approaches suffered from privacy concerns due to raw data collection and lacked mechanisms for dynamic result caching based on real-time network conditions. To address privacy concerns, \gls{FL} was introduced for collaborative task pattern prediction across fog servers without sharing raw computation data \cite{liu2020federated,lim2020federated}. These federated approaches maintain prediction accuracy while preserving privacy, but focus solely on prediction without integrating adaptive result caching strategies, limiting their effectiveness in dynamic scenarios.

Parallel to predictive approaches, \gls{RL} gained attention for intelligent caching decisions. Sadeghi et al. \cite{sadeghi2019deep} applied deep Q-learning to optimize caching in mobile fog computing, demonstrating superior performance in cache hit rate and latency. However, their work did not consider collaborative learning across distributed fog nodes. Wang et al. \cite{wang2020reinforcement} extended this with multi-agent RL for cooperative resource management, where multiple fog servers coordinate decisions through shared reward signals. While improving system performance, this approach required significant communication overhead and lacked predictive models for proactive caching. Li et al. \cite{li2024adaptive} introduced DRL-based adaptive cache management that dynamically adjusts policies based on task similarity patterns, showing promising latency reduction but operating centrally without collaborative learning.

\gls{SDN} emerged as a promising paradigm for centralized task orchestration. Xu et al. \cite{xu2018software} proposed an SDN-based framework where a centralized controller orchestrates task placement and resource allocation across distributed fog nodes, minimizing execution time while considering bandwidth and capacity constraints. However, centralized decision-making introduced scalability challenges and single points of failure. Zhang et al. \cite{zhang2019sdn} addressed scalability with hierarchical SDN architecture using distributed controllers for regional fog clusters, improving scalability but not incorporating machine learning for intelligent caching. Zhao et al. \cite{zhao2024distributed} introduced multi-agent approaches for distributed task management in SDN vehicular networks, though coordination mechanisms introduced additional latency.

Hybrid approaches combining multiple techniques have been explored. Li et al. \cite{li2020hybrid} proposed hybrid task offloading integrating pattern prediction with mobility-aware placement, where predicted similar tasks are proactively cached at fog nodes along vehicle trajectories, showing improvements in hit rate and completion time. However, the prediction model relied on centralized training, raising privacy concerns, and the caching strategy did not adapt to dynamic network conditions. He et al. \cite{he2021intelligent} combined \gls{FL} with fog computing but focused on task scheduling rather than result caching.

Recent hybrid FL-RL frameworks have emerged for intelligent caching systems. Wang et al. \cite{wang2024federated} proposed federated deep RL where distributed fog nodes collaboratively learn optimal caching policies while preserving privacy, demonstrating improved hit rates but requiring significant communication overhead for model aggregation. Kumar et al. \cite{kumar2024privacy} introduced privacy-preserving caching using \gls{FL} with differential privacy mechanisms, addressing security but not incorporating adaptive replacement based on real-time load and task patterns. Chen et al. \cite{chen2024energy} optimized energy efficiency through federated Q-learning, demonstrating substantial savings but struggling with large state spaces in complex scenarios.

Advanced hybrid frameworks have addressed comprehensive caching challenges. Zhang et al. \cite{zhang2025intelligent} integrated FL for task similarity prediction with DRL for intelligent cache replacement, demonstrating hybrid potential but focusing on static scenarios without fully addressing high mobility and intermittent connectivity. Ahmed et al. \cite{ahmed2025collaborative} extended this to autonomous vehicles with federated multi-agent RL for collaborative caching, though requiring frequent inter-agent communication introducing scalability challenges. Liu et al. \cite{liu2025predictive} incorporated transfer learning with \gls{FL} for predictive caching, leveraging transfer learning to accelerate convergence but maintaining static replacement strategies. Patel et al. \cite{patel2025dynamic} proposed hybrid FL-RL optimization for dynamic task result placement, jointly optimizing prediction and replacement but not fully addressing energy efficiency. Wu et al. \cite{wu2025secure} introduced blockchain-enabled federated RL ensuring data integrity and privacy, though blockchain integration introduced computational overhead limiting applicability in latency-sensitive applications.

Comprehensive surveys and frameworks have reviewed fog intelligence techniques. Zhou et al. \cite{zhou2019fog} reviewed \gls{FL}, transfer learning, and distributed optimization for vehicular networks, highlighting potential for privacy-preserving collaborative learning but not specifically addressing FL-RL integration for caching. Nguyen et al. \cite{nguyen2021federated} proposed federated RL for resource allocation in mobile fog computing, demonstrating the potential of combining FL and RL but focusing on computational resource allocation rather than cache management. Zhang et al. \cite{zhang2022collaborative} introduced collaborative caching using multi-agent RL where fog nodes learn cooperative policies through shared experiences, requiring frequent communication introducing overhead without leveraging \gls{FL} for privacy-preserving prediction.

Table \ref{table:comparison} presents a comprehensive comparison of existing approaches. Critical gaps remain in seamlessly integrating task similarity prediction with adaptive result caching strategies, achieving privacy-preserving collaborative learning with minimal communication overhead, enabling real-time adaptation to dynamic computational load and task patterns, and comprehensively optimizing cache hit rate, task completion time, and energy consumption simultaneously. Most existing works focus on either prediction (FL-based) or adaptation (RL-based) but not both in a unified framework, limiting their effectiveness in highly dynamic vehicular fog environments where both proactive task similarity prediction and reactive cache adaptation are essential for optimal task result management.

\section{Problem Statement and Motivation}

Vehicles in VANETs often encounter severe resource limitations when executing computational tasks locally \cite{wu2021design}. Modern vehicular applications—such as autonomous driving coordination, real-time hazard detection, cooperative traffic optimization, \hl{high-resolution video stream processing from dashboard cameras, real-time image recognition for pedestrian detection, LiDAR point cloud analysis for 3D environment mapping,} and sensor data processing—demand sophisticated processing capabilities that exceed the limited onboard computational resources, storage capacity, and battery power available in individual vehicles. This resource scarcity leads to increased task execution delays, higher failure rates for deadline-constrained tasks, and degraded quality of service for mission-critical applications. Consequently, task offloading to external computing infrastructure has become essential for meeting the stringent performance requirements of modern vehicular services.

To address these computational limitations, fog computing has emerged as a promising paradigm that deploys processing capabilities at \gls{RSU} and fog servers positioned throughout the network. By bringing computational resources closer to vehicles, fog computing reduces communication latency compared to traditional cloud-based solutions while providing sufficient processing power for complex vehicular applications. However, fog nodes themselves face resource constraints compared to cloud infrastructure, particularly during peak traffic periods in urban environments. This resource scarcity becomes critical when multiple vehicles repeatedly request identical computations\hl{, or when a single vehicle repeatedly offloads the same task multiple times}. For instance, during rush hour at busy intersections, dozens of vehicles may independently request the same traffic pattern analysis, route optimization for common destinations, or environmental data processing from overlapping sensor coverage areas. Each redundant execution consumes valuable computational resources and energy at fog nodes, preventing these resources from serving other critical tasks and leading to increased delays, higher failure rates, and degraded system performance.

Task result caching emerges as a promising solution by storing outputs of previously executed tasks and reusing them for subsequent identical requests. By preventing repetitive execution, caching conserves both energy and computational resources at fog nodes, freeing these resources for executing other critical tasks and improving overall system throughput. When a vehicle submits a task request, the fog node first queries its cache; upon finding a matching result, it is immediately returned without re-execution, dramatically reducing response time, energy consumption, and computational overhead. However, implementing effective task result caching in VANETs introduces three critical challenges that existing solutions fail to address comprehensively.

First, determining which task results to cache proactively requires predicting future task popularity patterns across distributed fog nodes. Without intelligent pre-placement strategies, cache misses force redundant re-execution, wasting computational resources and energy. Traditional frequency-based heuristics cannot capture the complex spatiotemporal dynamics of vehicular environments where task popularity fluctuates rapidly based on geographic location, traffic conditions, and time of day. A task frequently requested in one area may become obsolete within seconds as vehicles move to different locations. Moreover, certain urban areas such as commercial centers, administrative districts, and recreational zones typically experience higher traffic density and generate more computational tasks compared to residential areas, creating uneven demand patterns that simple caching policies cannot handle effectively.

Second, limited storage capacity at fog nodes necessitates intelligent eviction policies to determine which cached results to retain as new tasks continuously arrive. Static policies such as LRU, LFU, and FIFO rely on simple rules that fail to account for task heterogeneity—including varying priorities, deadlines, computational costs, and data sizes. Naive eviction may discard valuable results that will be requested again shortly, leading to cache thrashing and degraded performance. The challenge intensifies in VANETs where vehicle mobility creates highly dynamic access patterns; a task frequently requested in one area may become obsolete within seconds as vehicles move to different locations. Real-time eviction decisions must balance multiple competing objectives: maximizing cache hit rates, minimizing task completion times, ensuring deadline compliance for urgent tasks, and conserving energy for overall system efficiency.

Third, coordinating caching strategies across distributed fog infrastructure requires centralized visibility and control that traditional distributed approaches cannot provide. The SDN controller maintains a global view of network conditions, task distributions, and resource availability across all RSUs, enabling system-wide optimization. However, leveraging SDN for intelligent, learning-based cache management in vehicular fog computing remains largely unexplored. Existing SDN-VANET solutions focus primarily on routing optimization and load balancing, overlooking the potential for coordinated caching decisions to amplify overall system performance. Furthermore, while machine learning techniques offer promising approaches for adaptive caching, existing research predominantly explores \gls{FL} and \gls{RL} independently, failing to leverage their complementary strengths—FL for long-term pattern recognition and RL for short-term dynamic optimization.

In summary, effective task result caching in SDN-based VANETs requires a unified framework that addresses three key requirements: (1) proactive cache pre-placement based on predicted task popularity patterns, (2) adaptive real-time eviction policies that consider task heterogeneity and dynamic network conditions, and (3) coordinated decision-making across distributed fog nodes through centralized SDN control. Due to the lack of an existing study that addresses these requirements holistically, this paper proposes CoCa; a novel two-phase intelligent caching architecture that synergistically combines \gls{FL} for anticipatory cache population with \gls{RL} for reactive eviction control, achieving superior cache performance while reducing energy consumption and improving overall network efficiency.


\section{Network Architecture and System Model}

\subsection{SD-VANET Architecture}

This research proposes a hierarchical computing architecture for task result caching in VANETs, consisting of four layers:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Vehicle Layer:} This layer includes all moving vehicles in the urban area, equipped with wireless communication devices to interact with RSUs and nearby vehicles. In this layer, there are $N$ different vehicles, identified by numbers $i = 1, 2, \ldots, N$, distributed across the network. Each vehicle generates computational tasks that may require offloading to fog infrastructure.
    
    \item \textbf{Fog Layer:} This layer comprises $M$ \gls{RSU}, numbered $m = 1, 2, \ldots, M$, strategically positioned throughout the network. Each RSU is equipped with:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Computational Resources:} Processing capabilities for executing offloaded tasks from vehicles within coverage area.
        \item \textbf{Cache Storage:} Limited storage capacity $C_m$ for storing task results, enabling reuse of previously computed outputs.
        \item \textbf{Communication Interfaces:} Wireless links to vehicles, wired/wireless connections to other RSUs, and connection to the SDN controller.
    \end{itemize}
    
    
    \item \textbf{Control Layer:} Comprising a central SDN controller, this layer maintains a comprehensive view of network conditions, task distributions, cache states, and resource availability across all RSUs. The controller coordinates cache management decisions, including FL-based pre-placement and RL-based eviction policies.
\end{enumerate}

This architecture optimizes task processing and caching by leveraging distributed fog resources under centralized SDN control. Task $T_{ij}$, generated by vehicle $i$ with task identifier $j$, can be processed through one of the following mechanisms:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Cache Hit:} If a matching task result exists in the RSU's cache, it is immediately returned without re-execution, dramatically reducing response time, energy consumption, and computational overhead.
    
    \item \textbf{Local Fog Execution:} If no cached result exists and the RSU has sufficient computational resources, the task is executed locally at the fog node, and the result is stored in cache for future reuse.
    
    \item \textbf{Remote Fog Execution:} If the local RSU lacks resources, the SDN controller redirects the task to another RSU with available capacity, balancing load across the network.

\end{itemize}

The SDN controller, with comprehensive network awareness, coordinates the CoCa caching framework. The FL advisor analyzes task patterns across all RSUs and determines which results to pre-place in caches before requests arrive. The RL agent monitors real-time cache states and makes dynamic eviction decisions when storage capacity is reached. By integrating machine learning with SDN control, the system achieves intelligent cache management that adapts to dynamic vehicular patterns while maintaining global optimization objectives.

The network topology consists of multiple coverage regions, each served by an RSU with computational and caching capabilities. RSUs communicate with vehicles via wireless links and with each other through the SDN controller. Fig. \ref{fig:fl_rl_architecture} illustrates the CoCa architecture, showing the interaction between vehicles, RSUs, the FL advisor, the RL agent, and the SDN controller.

In this system, each vehicle first sends its task ($T_{ij}$) to the nearest RSU. The RSU checks its local cache for a matching result using a hash-based key derived from task intrinsics (task type, priority, data size). Upon cache hit, the result is immediately returned. Upon cache miss, the RSU forwards the task to the SDN controller, which schedules execution at an appropriate fog node based on resource availability and load balancing objectives. After execution, the result is stored in the cache and returned to the requesting vehicle. The FL advisor periodically analyzes task patterns and updates cache pre-placement decisions, while the RL agent continuously monitors cache states and makes eviction decisions when capacity limits are reached.

\subsection{Task Model}

Each task $T_{ij}$ generated by vehicle $i$ is characterized by the following attributes:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Task Type} ($\tau_{ij}$): Category identifier from set $\{0, 1, 2, 3\}$ representing different computational requirements (e.g., route optimization, traffic analysis, sensor processing, hazard detection).
    
    \item \textbf{Priority} ($p_{ij}$): Urgency level from set $\{0, 1, 2, 3\}$ with higher values indicating greater importance for mission-critical applications.
    
    \item \textbf{Data Size} ($s_{ij}$): Input payload size in kilobytes, affecting both transmission time and cache storage requirements.
    
    \item \textbf{Deadline} ($d_{ij}$): Hard time constraint by which task must complete. Tasks missing deadlines are considered failed.
    
    \item \textbf{Computational Requirement} ($c_{ij}$): CPU cycles needed for task execution, measured in megacycles.
    
    \item \textbf{Origin RSU} ($o_{ij}$): The RSU where task was submitted.
    
    \item \textbf{Destination RSU} ($dest_{ij}$): The RSU where vehicle expects to receive result, accounting for mobility.
\end{itemize}

Tasks are generated stochastically by vehicles with probability $\lambda$ per timestep. The task generation follows realistic patterns where certain task types are more frequent in specific geographic regions and time periods. For cache key generation, we use a hash function based on task intrinsics:

\begin{equation}
    key_{ij} = \text{hash}(\tau_{ij}, p_{ij}, \lfloor s_{ij}/100 \rfloor)
\end{equation}

This approach enables cache reuse across different vehicles requesting identical computations, as the cache key depends only on task characteristics, not vehicle identity.

\subsection{Cache Model}

Each RSU $m$ maintains a cache with the following properties:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Capacity} ($C_m$): Maximum number of task results that can be stored simultaneously.
    
    \item \textbf{Cache Entries}: Each entry stores $(key, result, metadata)$ where metadata includes:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Last access time (for LRU tracking)
        \item Access frequency (for LFU tracking)
        \item Time-to-live (TTL) value
        \item Task characteristics (type, priority, size)
    \end{itemize}
    
    \item \textbf{Eviction Policies}: The RL agent can select from five actions:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item $a_0$: No operation (maintain current state)
        \item $a_1$: Evict least recently used entry
        \item $a_2$: Evict random entry
        \item $a_3$: Evict least frequently used entry
        \item $a_4$: Shrink TTL (halve TTL of all entries)
    \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

Cache operations include:

\begin{equation}
    \text{Hit}(T_{ij}) = 
    \begin{cases}
        \text{True}, & \text{if } key_{ij} \in \text{Cache}_m \\
        \text{False}, & \text{otherwise}
    \end{cases}
\end{equation}

The cache hit rate is defined as:

\begin{equation}
    \text{HitRate} = \frac{\sum_{t=1}^{T} \text{Hits}_t}{\sum_{t=1}^{T} (\text{Hits}_t + \text{Misses}_t)}
\end{equation}

where $T$ is the total number of timesteps in the simulation.

\subsection{Delay Model}

To determine task execution delay within our architecture, we analyze three distinct scenarios:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Cache Hit}: If the task result exists in cache, the delay consists only of communication time:
    \begin{equation}
        t_{ij}^{\text{cache}} = t_{\text{trans},ij} + t_{\text{recv},ij}
    \end{equation}
    where $t_{\text{trans},ij}$ is the transmission time from vehicle to RSU, and $t_{\text{recv},ij}$ is the return time from RSU to vehicle.
    
    \item \textbf{Local Fog Execution}: For cache miss with local execution, the total time is:
    \begin{equation}
        t_{ij}^{\text{fog}} = t_{\text{trans},ij} + \frac{c_{ij}}{r_m} + t_{\text{cache\_store}} + t_{\text{recv},ij}
    \end{equation}
    where $r_m$ is the computational capacity of RSU $m$, and $t_{\text{cache\_store}}$ is the time to store result in cache (typically negligible).
    
    \item \textbf{Remote Fog Execution}: For execution at remote RSU $m'$:
    \begin{equation}
        t_{ij}^{\text{remote}} = t_{\text{trans},ij} + t_{\text{forward},mm'} + \frac{c_{ij}}{r_{m'}} + t_{\text{return},m'i} + t_{\text{recv},ij}
    \end{equation}
    where $t_{\text{forward},mm'}$ is inter-RSU communication time and $t_{\text{return},m'i}$ is result return time.
\end{itemize}

The transmission time from vehicle $i$ to RSU $m$ is calculated as:

\begin{equation}
    t_{\text{trans},ij} = \frac{s_{ij}}{B \log_2(1 + \text{SNR}_{im})}
\end{equation}

where $B$ is the channel bandwidth and $\text{SNR}_{im}$ is the signal-to-noise ratio between vehicle $i$ and RSU $m$.

\subsection{Energy Consumption Model}
\hl{The total energy consumption for processing task $\tau_{ij}$ (task $j$ from vehicle $i$) accounts for energy consumed at both the vehicle side and the fog side, comprising three components:}
\begin{equation}
    E_{ij} = E_{\text{comm},ij}^{\text{veh}} + E_{\text{comm},ij}^{\text{fog}} + E_{\text{comp},ij}^{\text{fog}}
\end{equation}
\hl{where $E_{\text{comm},ij}^{\text{veh}}$ is the vehicle's transmission energy for offloading the task to the RSU, $E_{\text{comm},ij}^{\text{fog}}$ is the fog node's transmission energy for delivering the result back to the vehicle, and $E_{\text{comp},ij}^{\text{fog}}$ is the computational energy consumed at the fog node for task execution.}
\hl{The vehicle-side communication energy for transmitting task data to the RSU is:}
\begin{equation}
    E_{\text{comm},ij}^{\text{veh}} = P_{\text{tx}}^{\text{veh}} \cdot t_{\text{trans},ij}
\end{equation}
\hl{where $P_{\text{tx}}^{\text{veh}}$ is the vehicle's transmission power and $t_{\text{trans},ij}$ is the task upload duration.}
\hl{The fog-side communication energy for returning the result to the vehicle is:}
\begin{equation}
    E_{\text{comm},ij}^{\text{fog}} = P_{\text{tx}}^{\text{fog}} \cdot t_{\text{recv},ij}
\end{equation}
\hl{where $P_{\text{tx}}^{\text{fog}}$ is the RSU's transmission power and $t_{\text{recv},ij}$ is the result delivery duration.}
\hl{For cache hits, computational energy is zero since no execution occurs at the fog node:}
\begin{equation}
    E_{\text{comp},ij}^{\text{fog}} = 
    \begin{cases}
        0, & \text{if cache hit} \\
        \kappa \cdot c_{ij}, & \text{if cache miss}
    \end{cases}
\end{equation}
where $\kappa$ is the energy coefficient per CPU cycle at the fog node.
\hl{Thus, the total network energy for processing all tasks is $E_{\text{total}} = \sum_{i} \sum_{j} E_{ij}$, capturing energy consumed by both vehicles (task offloading) and fog servers (result delivery and computation).} By preventing redundant task execution through intelligent caching, CoCa significantly reduces both communication and computational energy consumption. Cache hits eliminate the need for task execution, \hl{reducing $E_{\text{comp},ij}^{\text{fog}}$ to zero while only incurring the communication overhead ($E_{\text{comm},ij}^{\text{veh}} + E_{\text{comm},ij}^{\text{fog}}$), thereby} conserving fog node energy for other critical tasks and improving overall system efficiency.

\subsection{Performance Metrics}

The system performance is evaluated using the following metrics:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Cache Hit Rate}: Percentage of tasks served from cache without re-execution.
    
    \item \textbf{Task Success Rate}: Percentage of tasks completed before their deadlines.
    
    \item \textbf{Average Completion Time}: Mean time from task submission to result delivery.
    
    \item \textbf{Energy Savings}: Reduction in computational energy compared to no-caching baseline.
    
    \item \textbf{System Throughput}: Number of tasks successfully completed per unit time.
\end{itemize}

The optimization objective is to maximize cache hit rate and task success rate while minimizing average completion time and energy consumption, subject to cache capacity constraints and hard deadline requirements.
\section{Detailed Description of CoCa}

In this section, the CoCa caching mechanism is explained in detail, covering the two-phase architecture that combines \gls{FL} for proactive cache pre-placement with \gls{RL} for reactive cache eviction.
\subsection{Task Popularity Prediction using Federated Learning}

Efficient cache management in VANETs requires predicting which task results will be requested in the future to enable proactive pre-placement. Constantly querying all RSUs about their cache states would create significant network overhead and consume bandwidth needed for critical vehicular communications. This section proposes a \gls{FL}-based predictive model for estimating task popularity patterns across distributed fog nodes, thereby minimizing communication overhead while enabling intelligent cache pre-placement decisions.

The FL-based prediction process begins with feature engineering and distributed data collection across RSUs, followed by federated model training that preserves data privacy. Finally, the trained model predicts task popularity scores to guide cache pre-placement decisions.

To accurately predict task popularity, we developed a comprehensive 12-dimensional feature vector that captures multiple aspects of task characteristics, network conditions, and temporal dynamics. \subsubsection{Federated Learning Model Architecture}

The FL advisor employs a Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP) neural network architecture for task popularity prediction. The model architecture consists of:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Input Layer:} 12 neurons corresponding to the 12-dimensional feature vector
    \item \textbf{Hidden Layer 1:} 64 neurons with ReLU activation
    \item \textbf{Hidden Layer 2:} 32 neurons with ReLU activation
    \item \textbf{Output Layer:} 1 neuron with sigmoid activation, producing popularity score in [0,1] range
\end{itemize}

The \gls{FL} process follows the Federated Averaging (FedAvg) algorithm \cite{mcmahan2017communication}:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Initialization:} The SDN controller initializes global model parameters $\theta_0$
    \item \textbf{Local Training:} Each RSU $m$ trains the model on its local dataset $D_m$ for $E$ epochs:
    \begin{equation}
        \theta_m^{t+1} = \theta_m^t - \eta \nabla L(\theta_m^t; D_m)
    \end{equation}
    where $\eta$ is the learning rate and $L$ is the binary cross-entropy loss function
    \item \textbf{Model Aggregation:} The controller aggregates local models using weighted averaging:
    \begin{equation}
        \theta^{t+1} = \sum_{m=1}^{M} \frac{|D_m|}{|D|} \theta_m^{t+1}
    \end{equation}
    where $|D_m|$ is the size of local dataset at RSU $m$ and $|D| = \sum_{m=1}^{M} |D_m|$
    \item \textbf{Distribution:} The updated global model is distributed back to all RSUs
\end{enumerate}

This federated approach enables collaborative learning across RSUs while preserving data privacy, as raw task data never leaves individual fog nodes.

\subsection{Dynamic Cache Eviction using Reinforcement Learning}

While the FL advisor handles proactive cache pre-placement, real-time cache management during task execution requires adaptive eviction policies that respond to dynamic network conditions. The RL agent continuously monitors cache states and makes intelligent eviction decisions when storage capacity is reached, balancing multiple competing objectives such as maximizing cache hit rates, minimizing task completion times, and ensuring deadline compliance.

\subsubsection{State Space Representation}

The RL agent observes a 9-dimensional state space that captures the current cache status, network conditions, and task characteristics. The state vector $s_t$ at timestep $t$ is defined as:

\begin{equation}
s_t = [u_t, h_t, c_t, \bar{p}_t, \bar{s}_t, \bar{d}_t, l_t, q_t, a_t]
\end{equation}

where:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $u_t$: Cache usage ratio (occupied slots / total capacity)
    \item $h_t$: Current cache hit rate over recent window
    \item $c_t$: Average task completion time (normalized)
    \item $\bar{p}_t$: Mean priority of cached tasks
    \item $\bar{s}_t$: Mean data size of cached tasks (normalized)
    \item $\bar{d}_t$: Mean remaining TTL of cached entries (normalized)
    \item $l_t$: RSU computational load (percentage)
    \item $q_t$: Task queue length (number of pending tasks)
    \item $a_t$: Age of oldest cache entry (normalized)
\end{itemize}

All continuous features are normalized to [0,1] range to facilitate neural network training.

\subsubsection{Action Space Definition}

The RL agent selects from five discrete actions at each decision point:

\begin{equation}
\mathcal{A} = \{a_0, a_1, a_2, a_3, a_4\}
\end{equation}

where:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $a_0$: \textbf{No Operation} - Maintain current cache state
    \item $a_1$: \textbf{Evict LRU} - Remove least recently used entry
    \item $a_2$: \textbf{Evict Random} - Remove random entry (exploration)
    \item $a_3$: \textbf{Evict LFU} - Remove least frequently used entry
    \item $a_4$: \textbf{Shrink TTL} - Halve TTL of all entries (aging policy)
\end{itemize}

This action space provides flexibility for the agent to learn context-dependent eviction strategies rather than relying on a single fixed policy.

\subsubsection{Reward Function Design}

The reward function balances multiple objectives to guide the RL agent toward optimal caching decisions. At each timestep $t$, the reward $r_t$ is computed as:

\begin{equation}
r_t = w_h \cdot r_h + w_s \cdot r_s + w_c \cdot r_c + w_e \cdot r_e
\end{equation}

where the components are:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Cache Hit Reward} ($r_h$): 
    \begin{equation}
        r_h = \begin{cases}
            +3.0, & \text{if cache hit} \\
            -1.0, & \text{if cache miss}
        \end{cases}
    \end{equation}
    
    \item \textbf{Success Rate Reward} ($r_s$):
    \begin{equation}
        r_s = \begin{cases}
            +2.0, & \text{if task completed before deadline} \\
            -2.0, & \text{if task missed deadline}
        \end{cases}
    \end{equation}
    
    \item \textbf{Completion Time Reward} ($r_c$):
    \begin{equation}
        r_c = -\frac{t_{\text{completion}}}{t_{\text{deadline}}}
    \end{equation}
    
    \item \textbf{Eviction Penalty} ($r_e$):
    \begin{equation}
        r_e = \begin{cases}
            -0.5, & \text{if evicted high-priority task} \\
            0.0, & \text{otherwise}
        \end{cases}
    \end{equation}
\end{itemize}

The weights are set as $w_h = 3.0$, $w_s = 2.0$, $w_c = 1.0$, $w_e = 1.0$, emphasizing cache hit rate optimization while maintaining task success guarantees.

\subsubsection{Deep Q-Network Architecture}

The RL agent employs a Deep Q-Network (DQN) \cite{mnih2015human} to approximate the action-value function $Q(s, a; \theta)$. The DQN architecture consists of:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Input Layer:} 9 neurons (state space dimensions)
    \item \textbf{Hidden Layer 1:} 128 neurons with ReLU activation
    \item \textbf{Hidden Layer 2:} 64 neurons with ReLU activation
    \item \textbf{Output Layer:} 5 neurons (Q-values for each action)
\end{itemize}

The agent uses experience replay with buffer size 10,000 and $\epsilon$-greedy exploration with decay schedule:

\begin{equation}
\epsilon_t = \max(\epsilon_{\min}, \epsilon_{\max} \cdot \gamma^t)
\end{equation}

where $\epsilon_{\max} = 0.3$, $\epsilon_{\min} = 0.01$, and $\gamma = 0.995$.

The Q-learning update rule is:

\begin{equation}
Q(s_t, a_t) \leftarrow Q(s_t, a_t) + \alpha [r_t + \gamma \max_{a'} Q(s_{t+1}, a') - Q(s_t, a_t)]
\end{equation}

where $\alpha = 0.001$ is the learning rate.

\subsection{Integration of FL and RL Components}

The CoCa framework integrates both learning components within the SDN controller architecture. Algorithm \ref{algo:fl_rl_integration} describes the overall caching workflow.

\hl{The algorithm operates in two distinct phases that work synergistically to optimize cache management. In Phase 1 (lines 1-6), the FL advisor leverages collaborative learning across distributed RSUs to predict task popularity patterns based on historical features, proactively pre-placing the top-$k$ most promising task results in each RSU's cache before tasks arrive. This pre-placement strategy reduces cold-start cache misses and establishes a strong initial cache state. In Phase 2 (lines 7-30), the RL agent handles runtime cache management through dynamic eviction decisions. When a cache miss occurs and the cache is full (lines 14-24), the RL agent observes the current cache state (including occupancy, hit rates, and task characteristics), selects an eviction action using an $\epsilon$-greedy policy, and stores the new task result. The agent then receives a reward signal based on system performance metrics and updates its policy through Q-learning with experience replay (lines 21-23). Periodically (lines 32-36), federated averaging is performed across RSUs to update the global FL model. This dual-phase design ensures that FL captures long-term collaborative patterns through periodic federated averaging (every $T_{FL}$ timesteps), while RL continuously adapts to short-term dynamics and local conditions at each decision point, creating a robust and adaptive caching system that combines the strengths of both learning paradigms.}
\begin{algorithm}[t!]
\caption{CoCa Integrated Caching Framework}
\label{algo:fl_rl_integration}

\SetKwInOut{Input}{Input}
\SetKwInOut{Output}{Output}

\Input{Task stream $\mathcal{T}$, RSU set $\mathcal{M}$, Cache capacity $C$}
\Output{Cache hit rate, Task success rate, Avg completion time}

\Begin{
    Initialize FL advisor model $\theta_{FL}$ via federated training\;
    Initialize RL agent DQN $\theta_{RL}$ with random weights\;
    Initialize experience replay buffer $\mathcal{D}$\;
    
    \tcp{Phase 1: FL-based Pre-placement}
    \For{each RSU $m \in \mathcal{M}$}{
        Collect task history features $X_m$\;
        Predict popularity scores: $P_m = FL\_Advisor(X_m; \theta_{FL})$\;
        Pre-place top-$k$ tasks with highest scores in cache\;
    }
    
    \tcp{Phase 2: RL-based Dynamic Eviction}
    \For{each timestep $t = 1, 2, \ldots, T$}{
        \For{each incoming task $\tau_t$}{
            Check cache for matching result using hash key\;
            
            \If{cache hit}{
                Return cached result immediately\;
                Update hit rate statistics\;
            }
            \Else{
                Execute task at fog node\;
                
                \If{cache is full}{
                    Observe state $s_t$ from cache status\;
                    Select action $a_t$ using $\epsilon$-greedy policy\;
                    Execute eviction action $a_t$\;
                    Store new result in cache\;
                    Observe reward $r_t$ and next state $s_{t+1}$\;
                    Store transition $(s_t, a_t, r_t, s_{t+1})$ in $\mathcal{D}$\;
                    
                    \tcp{DQN Training}
                    Sample mini-batch from $\mathcal{D}$\;
                    Update $\theta_{RL}$ using Q-learning\;
                }
                \Else{
                    Store result directly in cache\;
                }
            }
        }
        
        Decay $\epsilon$ for exploration schedule\;
    }
    
    \tcp{Periodic FL Model Update}
    \If{$t \mod T_{FL} == 0$}{
        Perform federated averaging across RSUs\;
        Update global FL model $\theta_{FL}$\;
        Redistribute to all RSUs\;
    }
}
\end{algorithm}

The integration ensures that FL handles long-term popularity patterns through periodic model updates (e.g., every 100 timesteps), while RL continuously adapts to short-term dynamics through online learning at every decision point.

\subsection{Cache Key Generation and Result Matching}

To enable cache reuse across different vehicles requesting identical computations, CoCa uses a hash-based cache key generation mechanism that depends only on task intrinsics, not vehicle identity:

\begin{equation}
key(\tau_{ij}) = \text{hash}(\text{type}_{ij}, \text{priority}_{ij}, \lfloor \text{size}_{ij}/100 \rfloor)
\end{equation}

This approach allows multiple vehicles to benefit from cached results of previously executed identical tasks, maximizing cache utility and reducing redundant computations. The hash function ensures fast lookup with $O(1)$ complexity.

\subsection{Computational Complexity Analysis}

The computational complexity of CoCa components is analyzed as follows:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{FL Advisor Inference:} $O(d \cdot h_1 + h_1 \cdot h_2 + h_2)$ where $d=12$ is input dimension, $h_1=64$, $h_2=32$ are hidden layer sizes. This is negligible compared to task execution time.
    
    \item \textbf{RL Agent Decision:} $O(s \cdot h_1 + h_1 \cdot h_2 + h_2 \cdot a)$ where $s=9$ is state dimension, $a=5$ is action space size. Also negligible.
    
    \item \textbf{Cache Lookup:} $O(1)$ using hash-based indexing.
    
    \item \textbf{Federated Aggregation:} $O(M \cdot |\theta|)$ where $M$ is number of RSUs and $|\theta|$ is model parameter count. Performed periodically, not per-task.
\end{itemize}

The overhead introduced by CoCa is minimal compared to task execution times, making it practical for real-time VANET deployments.

\section{Experimental Setup and Performance Evaluation}

The SUMO simulation models a 5$\times$5 urban grid network with 200 vehicles over a 500-second simulation duration, generating realistic traffic patterns and task offloading demands. The fog computing simulator implements the SDN control unit, task scheduling logic, \gls{FL} models for task result prediction, and \gls{RL} agents for intelligent cache management.


To ensure statistical validity and reliability, each experimental scenario was executed over 30 independent simulation runs with different random seeds. The reported metrics represent average values across these runs, with 95\% confidence intervals calculated using Student's t-test. All performance differences reported are statistically significant (p $<$ 0.001) with narrow confidence intervals (±2-6\% coefficient of variation), demonstrating both measurement reliability and the robustness of our findings. This comprehensive benchmarking approach eliminates potential bias from individual simulation instances and provides the statistical rigor required for publication-quality technical evaluation.

The network handles 16,472 total task requests across all experimental scenarios, with each task representing a vehicle's computational offloading demand. \subsection{Baseline Methods}
To evaluate the effectiveness of CoCa, we conducted comprehensive comparative analyses against five baseline approaches:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{No Cache:} This serves as a baseline where no caching mechanism is employed. All tasks are computed from scratch without reusing previous results, representing the worst-case scenario for latency and computational overhead.
    
    \item \textbf{Random Caching:} This approach randomly selects task results to cache without considering any optimization criteria such as task similarity patterns or reuse probability. Cache replacement decisions are made arbitrarily.
    
    \item \textbf{\gls{LRU}:} A traditional caching policy that evicts the least recently accessed task result when the cache is full. This reactive approach does not consider future task patterns or result reusability predictions.
    
    \item \textbf{Least Frequently Used:} Another conventional caching strategy that removes the least frequently accessed task results based on historical access counts. While it considers access frequency, it lacks predictive capabilities and cannot adapt to changing task patterns.
    
    \item \textbf{FL-only:} This method employs \gls{FL} for collaborative task result prediction across RSUs but lacks the \gls{RL} component for dynamic cache replacement decisions \cite{liu2020federated}. It uses a simple replacement policy based on predicted task similarity without considering the dynamic network state and resource availability.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Performance Metrics}
We evaluate the performance of CoCa and baseline methods using four key metrics:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Task Success Rate:} The percentage of computational tasks that are successfully completed within their deadline constraints.
    \item \textbf{Cache Hit Rate:} The percentage of tasks whose results are found in cache (previously computed similar tasks), avoiding redundant computation.
    \item \textbf{Average Task Completion Time:} The mean time required to complete task execution, including computation and communication delays, measured in seconds.
    \item \textbf{Energy Consumption:} The total energy consumed by vehicles and fog servers for task offloading and execution, measured in Joules.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Results and Discussion}

\subsubsection{\textbf{Task Success Rate}}

The task success rate reflects the system's ability to complete computational tasks within their deadline constraints. CoCa achieves a success rate of 97.38\%, outperforming all baseline methods as shown in Figure \ref{fig:success_rate}. This represents improvements of 6.57 percentage points over FL-only (97.38\% vs. 90.81\%), 6.63 percentage points over LRU (97.38\% vs. 90.75\%), 6.59 percentage points over LFU (97.38\% vs. 90.79\%), 10.85 percentage points over Random (97.38\% vs. 86.53\%), and 64.04 percentage points over No Cache (97.38\% vs. 33.34\%).

\begin{figure}[t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.48\textwidth]{fig_success_rate.png}
    \caption{Task success rate comparison.}
    \label{fig:success_rate}
\end{figure}

The superior performance of CoCa is attributed to its intelligent cache management strategy that combines predictive task result modeling through \gls{FL} with adaptive cache replacement decisions via \gls{RL}. The FL component enables RSUs to collaboratively learn task similarity patterns without sharing raw computation data, while the RL agent dynamically adjusts cache replacement policies based on real-time network conditions, server load, and task characteristics. This synergy ensures that reusable task results remain cached and readily available, reducing computation time by 16.4\% and improving the success rate by 6.57 percentage points compared to FL-only.

When a cache hit occurs, the task can be completed almost instantly by retrieving the cached result, reducing completion time by 16.4\% compared to fresh computation. This time savings is crucial for meeting tight deadlines in vehicular fog computing scenarios. Traditional methods like LRU and LFU rely solely on historical access patterns and cannot anticipate future task similarities, resulting in suboptimal cache decisions. The FL-only approach, while capable of predicting task patterns, lacks the adaptive replacement mechanism needed to respond to dynamic network conditions and varying server loads.

\subsubsection{\textbf{Cache Hit Rate}}

Cache hit rate directly impacts both computation time and resource efficiency. A higher hit rate means more tasks can reuse previously computed results, eliminating redundant computation and reducing server load. CoCa achieves a cache hit rate of 95.48\% (15,728 hits out of 16,472 tasks), outperforming all baseline methods by 10.97 to 16.46 percentage points as illustrated in Figure \ref{fig:hit_rate}.

\begin{figure}[t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.48\textwidth]{fig_hit_rate.png}
    \caption{Cache hit rate comparison.}
    \label{fig:hit_rate}
\end{figure}

Specifically, CoCa improves the hit rate by 10.97 percentage points over FL-only (95.48\% vs. 84.51\%), 10.97 percentage points over LRU (95.48\% vs. 84.51\%), 10.97 percentage points over LFU (95.48\% vs. 84.51\%), and 16.46 percentage points over Random (95.48\% vs. 79.02\%).

The 95.48\% hit rate achieved by CoCa is a direct result of its dual-learning approach. The \gls{FL} component accurately predicts which task results will be requested in the near future by learning from collective task patterns across all RSUs. The \gls{RL} agent then uses these predictions, combined with real-time state information about server load and network conditions, to make optimal cache replacement decisions. This ensures that the most reusable task results remain cached, maximizing the probability of cache hits and minimizing redundant computation.

Notably, even the FL-only method, which uses predictive modeling, achieves 10.97 percentage points lower hit rate than CoCa (84.51\% vs. 95.48\%). This demonstrates that prediction alone is insufficient—adaptive replacement strategies that consider dynamic network state are essential for maximizing cache efficiency in vehicular fog computing environments where task patterns and network conditions change rapidly.

\subsubsection{\textbf{Average Task Completion Time}}

Average task completion time measures the latency experienced by vehicles when offloading computational tasks. Lower completion times indicate faster task execution and better user experience. CoCa achieves an average completion time of 2.50 ms as shown in Figure \ref{fig:completion_time}, representing a 16.4\% reduction compared to FL-only (2.50ms vs. 2.99ms), 16.3\% reduction compared to LRU (2.50ms vs. 2.99ms), 16.2\% reduction compared to LFU (2.50ms vs. 2.99ms), 22.8\% reduction compared to Random (2.50ms vs. 3.24ms), and 82.5\% reduction compared to No Cache (2.50ms vs. 14.31ms).

\begin{figure}[t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.48\textwidth]{fig_completion_time.png}
    \caption{Average task completion time comparison.}
    \label{fig:completion_time}
\end{figure}

The 16.4\% reduction in completion time is primarily driven by CoCa's 95.48\% cache hit rate. When a task result is available in cache, it can be retrieved almost instantaneously without the computational overhead associated with fresh task execution. The 95.48\% hit rate achieved by CoCa means that 15,728 out of 16,472 tasks benefit from cached results, resulting in 16.4\% lower average latency compared to FL-only.

The completion time includes several components: task transmission delay from vehicle to RSU, computation/cache retrieval time, and result delivery back to the vehicle. For cache hits, the computation component is replaced by a faster cache lookup operation (4-5ms vs. 10-50ms+ for fresh computation). Furthermore, CoCa's intelligent cache placement ensures that frequently needed task results are strategically distributed across RSUs based on predicted demand patterns, minimizing the average distance between requesting vehicles and cached results.

\subsubsection{\textbf{Energy Consumption}}

Energy efficiency is crucial in vehicular fog computing where both vehicles and fog servers have limited energy resources. CoCa achieves the lowest energy consumption at 3,708.80 Joules as presented in Figure \ref{fig:energy}, representing energy savings of 57.7\% compared to FL-only (3,708.80J vs. 8,763.60J), 57.8\% compared to LRU (3,708.80J vs. 8,778.60J), 57.7\% compared to LFU (3,708.80J vs. 8,769.60J), 67.9\% compared to Random (3,708.80J vs. 11,542.10J), and 86.5\% compared to No Cache (3,708.80J vs. 27,453.50J).

\begin{figure}[t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.48\textwidth]{fig_energy.png}
    \caption{Energy consumption comparison.}
    \label{fig:energy}
\end{figure}

The 57.7\% energy savings achieved by CoCa (3,708.80J vs. 8,763.60J compared to FL-only) are directly correlated with its 95.48\% cache hit rate and 16.4\% reduction in completion time. Cache hits require minimal energy as results are retrieved from memory without intensive computation. \hl{As formulated in Section IV, the energy model considers three components: (1) vehicle-side transmission energy $E_{\text{comm}}^{\text{veh}}$ for task offloading, (2) fog-side computational energy $E_{\text{comp}}^{\text{fog}}$ for task execution, and (3) fog-side communication energy $E_{\text{comm}}^{\text{fog}}$ for result delivery. For cache hits, $E_{\text{comp}}^{\text{fog}} = 0$, leaving only communication overhead, which explains the strong correlation between hit rate and energy savings.}

With a 95.48\% hit rate, CoCa requires only 744 fresh computations (cache misses) out of 16,472 tasks. In contrast, FL-only with 84.51\% hit rate requires 2,551 fresh computations (15.49\% miss rate), consuming 57.7\% more energy. The No Cache baseline represents the worst case, where every task requires full computation, resulting in maximum energy consumption of 27,453.50J. 

\hl{To contextualize these energy values, modern electric vehicles typically have battery capacities ranging from 40-100 kWh (144-360 MJ) {\cite{un2020comparison}}. CoCa's total energy consumption of 3,708.80J for processing 16,472 tasks represents only 0.001\% of a typical EV battery capacity (e.g., 60 kWh = 216 MJ), demonstrating that computational offloading energy overhead is negligible compared to vehicle propulsion energy. However, the 57.7\% energy savings achieved by CoCa compared to FL-only (5,054.80J reduction) becomes significant when aggregated across thousands of vehicles in urban deployments, reducing infrastructure energy costs and extending the operational lifetime of battery-powered RSU equipment in remote locations.}

CoCa's energy efficiency is particularly important for battery-powered vehicles and resource-constrained fog devices, extending operational lifetime and reducing overall system energy consumption.

\subsection{Key Findings and Insights}

By evaluating these key metrics comprehensively, we have demonstrated the effectiveness and efficiency of CoCa in optimizing task result caching in vehicular fog computing networks. The experimental results reveal several important insights:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Synergy of FL and RL:} The combination of \gls{FL} and \gls{RL} provides superior performance compared to using either approach alone. FL-only achieves better results than traditional methods but still falls short of CoCa by 6.57 percentage points in success rate and 10.97 percentage points in hit rate, demonstrating that adaptive replacement strategies are essential for dynamic vehicular environments.
    
    \item \textbf{Prediction Accuracy Matters:} CoCa's 95.48\% cache hit rate (15,728 hits out of 16,472 tasks) indicates that the \gls{FL} component accurately predicts task similarity patterns and result reusability, enabling proactive cache management that anticipates future task demands.
    
    \item \textbf{Adaptability is Crucial:} The RL agent's ability to dynamically adjust cache replacement decisions based on real-time network state, server load, and task characteristics ensures optimal performance under varying conditions and workloads, contributing to the 6.57 percentage point improvement in success rate.
    
    \item \textbf{Energy-Latency Trade-off:} CoCa achieves both 16.4\% lower latency and 57.7\% lower energy consumption simultaneously, demonstrating that intelligent result caching can optimize multiple objectives without compromise—a critical requirement for sustainable vehicular fog computing.
    
    \item \textbf{Consistent Superiority:} CoCa outperforms all baseline methods across all metrics with statistically significant improvements (p $<$ 0.001), indicating robust and reliable performance across diverse scenarios.
\end{enumerate}

Unlike existing methods that use only traditional caching policies (LRU, LFU) or standalone learning approaches (FL-only), CoCa integrates \gls{FL} for collaborative task pattern prediction with \gls{RL} for dynamic cache replacement decisions. While traditional methods rely on reactive policies based on historical access patterns, and FL-only uses predictive models without adaptive replacement, CoCa combines both prediction and adaptation for proactive, intelligent cache management across the distributed vehicular fog computing network. This hybrid approach enables CoCa to achieve 6.57 percentage points higher success rate, 10.97 percentage points higher hit rate, 16.4\% lower completion time, and 57.7\% lower energy consumption compared to the best baselines, making it an effective solution for vehicular fog computing task offloading scenarios.


\section{Conclusion}

This paper introduced CoCa, a novel hybrid intelligent caching framework that combines \gls{FL} for collaborative task pattern prediction with Deep \gls{RL} for adaptive cache replacement in Software-Defined VANETs. By synergistically integrating these complementary learning approaches within an SDN-coordinated architecture, CoCa addresses the critical challenge of redundant computation in vehicular fog computing while preserving data privacy and adapting to dynamic network conditions.

Our comprehensive experimental evaluation demonstrates substantial performance improvements across all key metrics. CoCa achieves a 95.48\% cache hit rate, 97.38\% task success rate, and 2.50ms average completion time---representing 10.97 percentage points higher hit rate, 6.57 percentage points higher success rate, 16.4\% lower completion time, and 57.7\% lower energy consumption compared to the best-performing baseline. These results validate that effective task result caching requires both proactive prediction and reactive adaptation---FL excels at privacy-preserving collaborative pattern learning across distributed RSUs, while RL enables dynamic eviction optimization under real-time constraints.

The key contributions of this work are fivefold. First, a novel hybrid FL+DRL caching framework is proposed that synergistically combines FL-based proactive cache pre-placement with DRL-based adaptive cache eviction, where FL captures long-term spatiotemporal popularity patterns and DRL reacts to short-term state fluctuations. Second, a vehicle-identity-agnostic task result caching mechanism is introduced using hash-based keys derived solely from task intrinsic parameters, enabling both intra-vehicle and cross-vehicle result reuse with $O(1)$ lookup complexity. Third, a distributed cache management architecture with SDN coordination is designed, implementing admission control, hot replication, and cross-RSU forwarding to maximize cache utilization under limited RSU storage. Fourth, a formal energy-aware caching optimization model is developed that captures vehicle-side transmission, fog-side communication, and fog-side computational energy components, enabling CoCa to jointly optimize cache performance and energy consumption by eliminating computational energy on cache hits. Fifth, comprehensive performance validation through systematic parameter optimization and comparative analysis against state-of-the-art baselines confirms the complementary contributions of both FL and DRL components. Our findings reveal that the integration of FL and RL provides complementary benefits neither achieves independently, and that task result caching differs fundamentally from content caching by requiring consideration of computational deadlines, task similarity, and energy efficiency.
